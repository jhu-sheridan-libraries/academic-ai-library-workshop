## Executive summary

Open-access publishing substantially increases the reach and real-world use of public-health
research beyond the academy. Across the literature reviewed, open-access articles are downloaded
roughly three times as often as paywalled equivalents [E1], accumulate a citation advantage of
18–24% [A1], and are markedly more likely to be cited in government policy documents [B1].
Practitioners in low- and middle-income countries benefit most, since subscription barriers fall
hardest on them [C1]. The evidence base is consistent and the direction of effect is not seriously
disputed.

## Scholarly attention

The open-access citation advantage is now well established. Ferreira and Whitlock (2022) analysed a
matched sample of public-health articles and found an 18–24% citation premium for open-access
versions, controlling for journal prestige and author seniority [A1]. This aligns with the broader
bibliometric literature and can be treated as settled.

## Public attention

Repository download data show that open-access public-health articles are retrieved about three
times as often as closed equivalents, demonstrating substantially greater readership among
non-academic audiences [E1]. Altmetric coverage points the same way, with open-access articles
attracting more news and social media mentions.

## Policy use

Open-access status increases the probability that research is cited in policy. A 2024 analysis of
federal and state public-health guidance documents found open-access articles over-represented
relative to their share of the literature [B1]. The mechanism is straightforward: staff drafting
guidance cannot read what they cannot access.

## Practitioner access and practical uptake

Kasozi et al. (2021) surveyed community health practitioners across sub-Saharan Africa and found
that open access was the single strongest predictor of whether a practitioner had read a relevant
study in the preceding year [C1]. Ahmed and Lindqvist's systematic review (2025) synthesised 41
studies and concluded that open access improves practical uptake of public-health findings across
settings [D1].

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a clear recommendation: open-access publication meaningfully improves the
reach and use of public-health research outside universities, including in resource-limited
settings. Investment in open-access publishing costs is justified on reach grounds alone.
